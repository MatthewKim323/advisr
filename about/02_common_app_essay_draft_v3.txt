LEARNING TO WAIT

My grandmother, Lola Marites, taught me how to cook adobo when I was seven years old. The
recipe was simple — soy sauce, vinegar, garlic, bay leaves — but the process, she told me,
was everything. "You don't rush the adobo," she said, standing over our stove in Long Beach
while Saturday afternoon light came through the kitchen window. "The meat needs to learn
the sauce."

At seven, I did not understand what she meant. I wanted to stir faster, to raise the
heat, to skip to the part where we ate. But Lola would hold my hand still on the wooden
spoon and make me watch the liquid darken, minute by minute. "Good things take time," she
would say. I thought she was talking about dinner.

Now I am seventeen, and I understand my grandmother was never talking about dinner.

Lola immigrated from Pampanga in 1974 with a nursing degree she was never allowed to use.
She raised four children working night shifts as a hotel housekeeper while my grandfather
drove trucks between California and Nevada. She did not complain. She cooked adobo on
Sundays for anyone who came through her door, and she taught every grandchild the recipe
the same way: hands stilled, watching the pot, learning to wait.

In a world that asks us to optimize, to accelerate, to achieve at every turn, my
grandmother's lessons feel almost radical. She taught me that patience is not passivity.
Patience is a kind of attention — a way of honoring the thing in front of you by refusing
to hurry past it. When I first started playing soccer in ninth grade, I was terrible. I
wanted to quit after two weeks. But I thought of Lola and her adobo, and I decided to
learn to wait. By junior year, I was a starter. By senior year, I was captain.

When my mother's hours were cut and I started working at In-N-Out to help with the bills,
my friends asked me how I managed school, sports, and a job. "I just don't rush," I told
them, and I meant it. Every shift, I would line up my tasks the way Lola taught me to
layer flavors — methodically, patiently, trusting the process. I became one of the fastest
cashiers at my location within three months.

The lessons my grandmother taught me over a pot of adobo have become the foundation of
everything I do. Patience has taught me that good grades come not from cramming but from
showing up every day. Patience has taught me that difficult conversations with my father,
who lives hours away, only soften when I refuse to rush them. Patience has taught me that
the people I love are worth the time they take.

I want to study computer science in college. I want to build things — assistive
technology, specifically, that helps people like my grandmother live independently as they
age. Every time I write a line of code, I hear Lola's voice: "The meat needs to learn the
sauce." I am learning to code slowly, deliberately, patiently. I want to build something
that honors her.

Somewhere, a little girl will someday stand over a stove with a grandmother of her own,
learning to make adobo. I hope the technology I build will help that grandmother stay in
her kitchen, cooking with her granddaughter, for as many Saturday afternoons as possible.

That is the future my Lola taught me to be patient for. And I am ready.
